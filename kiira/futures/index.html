What happens when thousands of people, operators, boda riders and shops each make their own choice?

This lab rebuilds the Kiira model as an agent-based model. Households pick how to travel each week, operators learn fares and fleet sizes, boda riders cut prices or leave, and shops decide whether to join a delivery aggregator. The model runs every option for all 100 suburbs over many uncertain futures, then ranks the best three options for each area by what you care about.

The agents

Nothing below is fixed in advance. Ridership, fares, fleet size, boda earnings and orders come out of the agents' choices, week by week, over two years. The network model on the main page finds the best fares in one step. Here people and firms learn, copy each other and react.

Households90 sample families per suburbIncome from Meta's wealth index, a distance to the nearest stop, their own value of time and their own trust in new things. Each week they choose walk, boda or feeder for a peak trip and an off-peak trip. They hear about the feeder from adverts and neighbours, remember crowding and being left at the stop, and tend to stick with what they used last week.
OperatorOne per suburbLaunches with 85% of its planned fleet. Each month it nudges fares up or down in each time period and keeps changes that helped. It orders vehicles when buses are full or riders are refused (8-week delivery) and releases them when buses run empty.
Boda ridersA pool per suburbTheir daily earnings follow the trips left to them. When pay falls below what they could earn elsewhere, they cut fares and some leave. Leavers fill shuttle-driver and courier jobs first.
Shops and buyersLocal delivery aggregatorShops join when expected orders beat the commission. Households start ordering once enough nearby shops are listed. Feeders carry parcels in off-peak hours and couriers walk or e-bike the last few hundred metres.
Kiira plantSupplierElectric fleets wait in a queue for plant output (40% of 2,500 a year at first). Diesel taxis are imported, so they can start at once.

How families are affected

The gain per household combines money and time: shorter walks, less waiting, cheaper trips than a boda and fewer shopping trips, minus fares that replace walking. It is the standard "logsum" measure from the choice model, counted in shillings a month. Bodas lose trips, so their riders' families are shown separately.

A local delivery aggregator

Nearby shops list their goods on one app. Orders go into totes that ride the feeder in quiet hours, and couriers on foot or e-bike carry them the last few hundred metres. Families save a shopping trip, shops gain sales, couriers gain work, and the operator fills empty seats.

The aggregator raises welfare and jobs in almost every suburb, so it is in nearly every best option. With a low commission or few orders, the platform's own fixed costs can exceed its fees in small suburbs (try the commission slider and re-run). Some sales move from other shops, so shop jobs are counted net of that.

How to use this lab

Method and uncertainty

Each of the 100 suburbs is simulated for 104 weeks with 90 sample households. Each household stands for a few hundred real ones. Trips, costs, walking, waiting, boda fares and the logit choice use the same formulas and inputs as the network model on the main page. With the same fare and fleet, the two models agree on daily riders to within about 5%.

What the agents add. Awareness spreads by Bass diffusion (adverts plus word of mouth). Habit is a bonus in shillings for last week's mode. Refusals at full buses add remembered waiting time. Operators learn fares by trial and error each month and adjust fleets with a delivery lag. Boda riders' pay responds to lost trips, leading to price cuts and exits. Shop and household adoption of the aggregator feed each other, and plant output is shared across suburbs.

Uncertainty. Each future draws all twelve uncertain inputs (table) at once. Every option in a suburb faces the same futures and the same families, so differences between options come from the policy, not from luck. Results show the median and the 10th to 90th percentile.

Scoring. For each suburb, every option's median family gain, net jobs, money (operator NPV plus platform profit minus city subsidy over 10 years), robustness (the same at the 10th percentile) and CO₂ saved are scaled from 0 to 1 against the other options there. The weighted average ranks them.

Limits. All inputs are planning assumptions. Replace them with household surveys, operator data and Kiira Motors costs. The 14-seat e-shuttle is a product concept, not a current Kiira model. Trunk buses, taxis' own response and land use are not agents here.
